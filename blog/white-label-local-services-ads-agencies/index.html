If you run an SEO, PPC or local marketing agency, you’ve probably had a client ask about the ads with the “Google Verified” badge at the top of the results. Those are Local Services Ads (LSA). They’re pay-per-lead, they sit above regular search ads for most home service searches, and clients like them because they pay for phone calls and messages, not clicks.

Offering LSA sounds easy: set up the account, set a budget, send a report. In practice the setup is the small part. Whether LSA works for a client depends mostly on what happens when the phone rings, and that part sits outside Google Ads entirely. This guide walks through the real job so you can price it, staff it or partner for it with your eyes open.

What LSA looks like for agencies now

Google is moving Local Services Ads out of the standalone LSA dashboard and into Google Ads, as Performance Max campaigns with pay-per-lead goals. The pay-per-lead model stays: advertisers still pay for valid leads such as calls and messages, not clicks. Source: Google Ads Help

Google is moving accounts in waves: select US home and storefront service categories from August 2026, broader groups including service-area businesses without storefronts in late 2026, and non-US accounts and remaining categories in 2027. A few changes matter for agencies right away:

  • New clients still start outside Google Ads. Google says you can’t create new pay-per-lead campaigns directly in Google Ads yet; new accounts still sign up at g.co/localservices.
  • Manual bidding is gone after migration. Manual max cost-per-lead isn’t supported, and weekly budgets become a daily average (the weekly budget divided by 7).
  • Performance history doesn’t move. Past impressions, clicks, weekly spend and ad-level reports don’t migrate, so save them first.
  • The account administrator gets the notice. Google emails the admin 14 days before migration. If that’s the client’s personal inbox, you may never see it.

Three of those rows changed recently, and each one adds work:

  • Missed calls can be billed. Google lists a charged lead as one where the business misses a phone call during business hours and the customer stays on the line for more than 20 seconds. Source: Google Local Services Help See how to check a client’s bill for missed-call charges.
  • Credits are automated. Google now handles credits automatically, driven by lead feedback, and no longer credits leads for “job type not serviced” or “geo not serviced.” Source: Google Local Services Help Settings and lead ratings matter more than dispute requests now.
  • Business Profile edits can pause the campaign. After migration, significant changes to a client’s business name, storefront address or primary category trigger a verification review that typically takes 24 to 48 hours, and the campaign may pause while it runs. Source: Google Ads Help

Lead response is the part agencies underestimate

An agency controls the account. It doesn’t control the client’s front desk. That gap is where most LSA programs leak:

  • The owner is on a roof when the call comes in, and the call goes to voicemail.
  • A message lead sits in an inbox nobody checks until the next morning.
  • The lead email alert goes to a shared address that everyone has learned to ignore.

Each of those is a lead the client paid for or a customer who called the next business on the list. And the client won’t blame their front desk. They’ll say “LSA doesn’t work” and cancel your retainer. Our guides on responding to message leads and lead notifications cover the mechanics.

So the real decision when you add LSA to your menu isn’t which bid strategy to use. It’s who answers every lead, for every client, all day, and how you’ll prove it in the report.

Running many clients: accounts, access and ownership

A few rules keep a multi-client LSA book clean.

One client, one account

Google’s third-party policy requires a separate account for each end advertiser you manage. Source: Google Advertising Policies Help Don’t run two clients through one account to save setup time.

Link everything to your manager account

Google says LSA manager accounts are set up in Google Ads, and linking to a manager account is required for features like consolidated monthly billing. Source: Google Local Services Help Our manager account guide walks through linking and access levels.

Decide who gets lead alerts

After migration, lead email alerts are controlled under Admin › Notifications in Google Ads, and Google opts all account users and direct managers in by default. Each account needs at least one user who stays opted in. Source: Google Ads Help With twenty clients and five team members, that default becomes a flood of emails that people start ignoring. Pick one named alert owner per client, plus a backup, and switch everyone else off.

Put ownership in writing

Some agencies own the LSA account. Some clients insist on owning it. Either can work. What doesn’t work is finding out at cancellation. Write down who owns the account, whose card pays for leads, and what happens to access and history if the client leaves.

Building makes sense if you already run a call center or only manage a handful of big accounts. Partnering makes sense when you want LSA as a repeatable product across many clients without hiring a team to sit on the phones.

Whoever you choose, ask them three questions: does it work on accounts that have moved to Google Ads, can it keep each client fully separate, and can it run under your brand?

Stay on the right side of Google’s third-party rules

When you buy and manage ads for clients, Google’s third-party policies apply to you. The ones that matter for LSA pricing and reporting: Source: Google Advertising Policies Help

  • Disclose your management fee. If you charge a fee separate from the cost of Google Ads, tell new customers in writing before their first purchase and show it on every invoice.
  • Report Google’s cost exactly. When you share Google advertising cost data, report the exact amount Google charged, not including your fees.
  • Hand over the customer ID when asked. Clients must be able to get their Google Ads customer ID from you so they can contact Google directly.

A rollout plan for your first client

  1. Pick a client who already runs LSA and complains about lead quality or missed calls. You’ll have a before-and-after to show.
  2. Audit the account: who the admin is, who gets lead alerts, whether the routing number reaches a person, whether it has migrated, and last month’s charged leads.
  3. Fix the response side first. Answering and message replies move results faster than bid changes.
  4. Agree on ownership, alerts and reporting in writing.
  5. Report on outcomes after 30 days: leads answered, booked and credited, not just leads received. Then roll the same setup out to the next client.

Frequently asked questions

What is white-label Local Services Ads management?

It means a partner provides the LSA work or software behind your agency, and your agency stays the brand the client sees. You keep the client relationship and set your own price.

Can agencies still create new Local Services Ads accounts after the move to Google Ads?

Yes, but not inside Google Ads yet. Google says new pay-per-lead campaigns can’t be created directly in Google Ads at this time, so new accounts still start at g.co/localservices.

Do I need a separate Google Ads account for each LSA client?

Yes. Google’s third-party policy requires a separate account for each end advertiser you manage. Link each one to your manager account.

Should my agency or my client own the LSA account?

Either can work. Put it in writing at the start: who owns the account, whose card pays for leads, and what happens to access and history if the client leaves.
