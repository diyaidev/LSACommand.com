Google Now Charges for Missed Calls: How to Check Your LSA Bill

Since October 1, Google Local Services Ads can bill you for a phone call nobody at your company answered. October was the first full month under the rule. Here’s exactly what Google charges for, how to find those charges on your bill, and six fixes that stop you paying for leads you never spoke to.

Local Services Ads have always been pay-per-lead: you pay when a real customer contacts you, not when someone sees your ad. What changed on October 1, 2026 is what counts as “contact.” A call can now be a charged lead even if it rings out and nobody at your business ever speaks to the customer.

If you run LSA, or you run it for clients, your October bill is the first full month with these charges on it. This guide walks through the rule, the details people miss, a worked example of what it can cost, and how to audit the charges against your own call log.

The rule, in Google’s words

Google’s help page on how leads work now lists this as a charged lead: “You missed a phone call during your business hours and the customer stays on the line for more than 20 seconds.” Source: Google Local Services Help

Nobody has to pick up and nobody has to call back. Three conditions have to be true:

  1. It’s during your business hours. Google means the hours on your Google Business Profile, not the hours you think you’re open.
  2. It’s inside your ad schedule, if you use one. If your ad schedule differs from your business hours, Google only charges an unanswered call that lands inside both.
  3. The caller stays on the line more than 20 seconds.

Four details that decide whether a missed call is charged

1. Voicemail doesn’t protect you

Google says a call that goes to voicemail or an automated greeting during business hours is treated as a connected call. It’s charged after 20 seconds whether or not the caller leaves a message. If your plan was “it’s fine, they’ll leave a voicemail,” that plan now costs a lead charge every time.

2. Press-1 menus change when the clock starts

If your phone system makes callers press a key to be routed, the 20-second timer starts when they press their first key. If they hang up without pressing anything, you aren’t charged, even if the menu played for a full minute. Extra key presses in a multi-level menu don’t reset the timer again.

3. Answered calls follow the normal rules

The 20-second rule only applies to unanswered calls. If someone at your business answers, even after a long ring, you aren’t charged based on ring time; the call is judged the normal way. So this rule hits one thing: calls nobody picks up.

4. Repeat callers have a 15-day window

Once a customer has been charged as a lead, follow-up calls or messages from the same phone number or email aren’t charged again, as long as there’s been contact within 15 days. If 15 days pass with nothing, the next call can be a brand-new charge.

What missed calls can cost

“A few missed calls” sounds small. Two data points say otherwise. Invoca’s research, based on more than 60 million calls, found that 27% of calls to home services businesses go unanswered. And ServiceTitan reported that in its data, 65.9% of abandoned LSA calls lasted at least 20 seconds. Most missed calls clear Google’s bar.

How to find missed-call charges on your October bill

  1. Open your lead inbox. If the account has already moved into Google Ads, it’s the Leads page under Goals › Conversions.
  2. Filter to phone leads charged in October.
  3. Look for three patterns: charged calls with no conversation in the recording, just ringing or your voicemail greeting; charged calls at times nobody was on the phones, like lunch, shift change or the last hour of the day; and the same number showing up again after a gap of more than 15 days.
  4. Pull your own phone system’s call log for the same dates and line the two up. Every charged call with no matching answered call on your side is a missed-call charge.
  5. Write down three numbers: how many, what they cost, and what time of day they happened. The time of day tells you which fix to start with.

A simple sheet with Date, Time, Charged?, Answered on our side? and Cost columns is enough.

Six fixes, in the order we’d do them

1. Make your hours honest

Your Business Profile hours are now a billing setting. If your profile says open until 7 and the office goes home at 5, every call from 5 to 7 that rings out is a charge. Either cover those hours or change them. Longer hours can help you show up more, but only if someone or something actually answers. If your account has migrated, Google says hours changes on your Business Profile sync to Google Ads within 24 hours. Source: Google Ads Help

2. Line up your ad schedule

If you use an ad schedule, the charge window is where the schedule and your business hours overlap. Make that overlap match the hours you can really cover.

3. Put an overflow on the line

Ring your team first. If nobody picks up in about 15 seconds, roll the call to something that answers: an answering service, a second person, or an AI receptionist. To be clear, this isn’t about dodging Google’s charge; an answered call can still be a charged lead. The point is that if you’re paying for the lead anyway, you should get the customer’s name, the job, and a booked appointment for it.

4. Stop treating voicemail as a safety net

Voicemail is now the most expensive way to miss a call. If it’s your overflow, replace it.

5. Block the robocalls

A spam call that rings out on an unanswered line is exactly what this rule catches, and advertisers have posted about being charged for robocalls. Block spam at the carrier or call-routing level before it ever rings.

6. Rate every single lead

Manual disputes are gone. Google moved to automated lead credits in 2024 and no longer credits “job type not serviced” or “outside service area” leads. Source: Google Local Services Help What’s left is the lead feedback survey. Rate every lead, good and bad: Google says the feedback helps it send you better leads, and it can earn a bonus credit. It’s a few clicks per lead, and almost nobody does it consistently. Our guide to bad LSA leads covers the rest.

Frequently asked questions

Does Google charge for missed calls on Local Services Ads?

Yes, since October 1, 2026. Google’s help center lists a missed call during your business hours where the customer stays on the line for more than 20 seconds as a charged lead, even if nobody answers or calls back.

Am I charged if a missed LSA call goes to voicemail?

Yes. Google treats a call that reaches voicemail or an automated greeting during business hours as a connected call, and charges it if the caller stays on the line for more than 20 seconds, whether or not they leave a message.

What counts as business hours for LSA missed-call charges?

The hours on your Google Business Profile. If you also use an ad schedule that differs from those hours, an unanswered call is only charged when it lands inside both your active ad schedule and your business hours.

Does a press-1 phone menu change the 20-second rule?

Yes. If your phone system asks callers to press a key, the 20-second timer starts when they press their first key. If they hang up without pressing a key, Google says you aren’t charged, even if the menu played for more than 20 seconds.

Can I dispute a charged missed call?

Manual disputes are gone; Google moved to automated lead credits. What you can still do is rate every lead through the lead feedback survey, which Google says helps it send better leads. The reliable fix is answering the calls you are paying for.
