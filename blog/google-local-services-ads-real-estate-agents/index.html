Google Local Services Ads for Real Estate Agents: A 2026 Guide for Agents and Their Marketers

Real estate is one of the professional categories on Local Services Ads, and it plays by different rules from home services. Here’s how screening works for agents and brokers, the targeting limits that come with housing ads, how leads are charged, and how agencies set it up for real estate clients.

Most writing about Local Services Ads (LSA) is about plumbers, roofers and HVAC. Real estate agents can run LSA too, and marketing agencies that serve agents and brokerages get asked about it all the time.

The catch is that real estate is a professional category with its own screening, and it’s housing advertising, which comes with targeting limits. This guide covers what Google checks, what you can and can’t target, how leads work, and how to set it up for a client. It reflects Google’s help pages as of November 2, 2026.

Is real estate on Local Services Ads?

Yes. Google’s US screening table has a category called Real estate agents and brokers, described as agents who “help buy, sell, or rent property.” It sits under Professional Services with lawyers. Source: Google Local Services Help

LSA is only offered for certain categories and areas, so check eligibility for the client’s location before you promise anything. Source: Google Local Services Help Our LSA categories guide explains how to check.

Home Listings Ads. Since June 2026, Google has been rolling out Local Services Ads for Home Listings across all 50 US states. When buyers search phrases like “homes for sale near me,” the ad can show for-sale listings (price, photos, listing agent and neighborhood data, supplied by HouseCanary’s ComeHome) alongside promoted buyer’s agents, and buyers can call or message an agent from it. Source: Google Google says an agent gets the format automatically by opting into the Buyer’s agent or Seller’s agent job types in the Real estate category, with a verified Business Profile and an active LSA account that has passed Google’s standard verification. It runs on mobile in US markets, doesn’t show on Google Maps, and is still pay per lead. Source: Google Local Services Help

What Google checks for agents and brokers

These are the screening requirements Google lists for the real estate category: Source: Google Local Services Help

CheckWhat Google lists
IdentityBusiness owner identity verification
BackgroundBusiness check (select users only)
Business registrationBusiness check and business representative check (select users only)
InsuranceProfessional liability insurance, where applicable by local law
LicenseLicense checks for each real estate agent in the firm

Two details matter for brokerages:

  • Every agent’s license is checked. A team or brokerage with many agents needs each one’s license details ready. Plan for that before sign-up, not after.
  • Some states skip the separate background check. Google says that in states where agents must pass a background check to get a license, Local Services doesn’t run its own. Those states are Alaska, Arkansas, Connecticut, Delaware, Hawaii, Idaho, Indiana, Kentucky, Maine, Maryland, Massachusetts, Michigan, Montana, New Hampshire, New Mexico, New York, North Carolina, North Dakota, Ohio, Pennsylvania and Vermont.

Which badge shows

Google’s screening page still refers to the “Google Screened” badge for real estate agents. Google’s newer pages describe a single Google Verified badge shown next to the business name “or featured professionals.” Source: Google Local Services Help We cover that change in Google Verified badge: what replaced Google Guaranteed. Google says it has replaced its advertiser badges with the single Google Verified badge for all advertisers, so use “Google Verified,” not “Google Screened,” in the client’s marketing. Source: Google Local Services Help

Going live before the badge

Google offers pre-badge ads, so a business can get leads after it passes preliminary checks and before onboarding is finished. For professional categories, Google lists these requirements: Source: Google Local Services Help

  • a license (or business registration where no license is submitted)
  • headshots
  • reviews, depending on the category
  • completed billing setup and a budget

Pre-badge listings rank below providers who have finished onboarding and earned the badge. Have good headshots ready on day one, because they’re a requirement here, not decoration.

Targeting rules for housing ads

This is the part that catches marketers coming from Meta or Search campaigns.

  • No ZIP code targeting. Google says “Real estate agents and brokers in the US and Canada can’t target their ads by postal code.” Source: Google Local Services Help Build the service area from cities and counties.
  • No demographic targeting. Under Google’s personalized ads policy, housing is an “access to opportunities” category in the US and Canada. Housing ads can’t target by gender, age, parental status or marital status, and can’t use ZIP code location targeting. Source: Google Advertising Policies Help

In practice, real estate LSA targeting comes down to three things: the category, the job types the client really handles and the cities or counties they serve. Keep each one accurate. Google’s LSA policy says advertisers can’t misrepresent their service areas. If a license only covers a certain area, the ad shouldn’t claim to serve outside it. Source: Google Advertising Policies Help

How real estate leads are charged

Real estate leads follow the same rules as every other category. The client pays per valid lead, not per click. Google counts these as valid leads: Source: Google Ads Help

  • an answered call, or a call back where you speak with the customer
  • a missed call during business hours where the caller stays on the line for more than 20 seconds
  • a text, email or voicemail
  • a booking request (US and Canada)

Follow-up contact from the same person within 15 days isn’t charged again. Google screens out invalid leads and can automatically credit low-quality ones. Source: Google Local Services Help

Two habits help agents here:

  • Set Business Profile hours honestly. Google treats those hours as “business hours” when it decides whether a missed call is charged. An agent who lists 8 a.m. to 8 p.m. and doesn’t answer at 7 p.m. pays for those missed calls.
  • Answer fast. Google says responsiveness affects ranking and missed calls “may negatively affect your responsiveness.” Message and booking leads help “during nights and weekends when you can’t answer the phone.” Source: Google Local Services Help Agents are often at showings, so this is the hard part for them.

For how billing and payment thresholds work, see Google Local Services Ads billing.

ILLUSTRATION: does a lead pay off?

Real estate lead values are lumpy because one closing pays far more than one service call. The math is still the same three numbers: cost per lead, how many leads become clients, and what a closed deal is worth. ILLUSTRATION only, not real data: if leads cost $60 each, and 1 in 25 leads closes a deal worth $6,000 in commission to the agent, the agent spends about $1,500 per closing. That’s a strong return. If only 1 in 100 closes, it’s $6,000 per closing, which wipes out the commission. Plug in the client’s own numbers. For how agencies run this math, see are Google Local Services Ads worth it.

The referral-fee trap

Real estate runs on referrals, and agents routinely pass a lead to an agent in another market for a referral fee. With LSA leads, that can break Google’s rules.

Google’s Local Services policy says advertisers “cannot sell or pass leads to other businesses.” It also says leads are “only to be fulfilled by you or the vetted technicians of your company,” and that “consumers should know exactly who to expect.” Google’s examples include “passing along or selling a lead to another business.” Source: Google Advertising Policies Help

For a brokerage, the safe reading is to keep LSA leads inside the firm named in the ad. Don’t route them to outside agents or a referral network. Agree this with the client in writing before launch. A suspension takes down every ad in the account, and Google says related accounts can be permanently suspended. Source: Google Advertising Policies Help

Brokerage or individual agent?

Google’s screening is set up for a firm with agents in it: identity verification for the owner, and license checks “for each real estate agent in the firm.” Source: Google Local Services Help Before you set up, settle a few things with the client:

  1. Whose name is on the ad. It has to match the business customers will deal with, and replies have to use the same name. Source: Google Advertising Policies Help
  2. Who answers. If several agents share the leads, decide the routing before launch so no lead sits.
  3. Who owns the account. Put it in writing, as with any client. See how to manage client LSA from a manager account.
  4. Separate accounts for separate businesses. Google doesn’t allow duplicate accounts to get more traffic, Source: Google Advertising Policies Help and agencies need a separate account for each end advertiser. Source: Google Advertising Policies Help

A setup checklist for agencies

  1. Check that the category is available in the client’s area.
  2. Collect license details for every agent in the firm, plus insurance if local law requires it.
  3. Get professional headshots and the client’s existing reviews ready.
  4. Clean up the Google Business Profile, with accurate name, hours and category.
  5. Build the service area from cities and counties. No ZIP codes.
  6. Turn on only the job types the client actually handles, including Buyer’s agent and Seller’s agent if they want Home Listings Ads.
  7. Agree lead routing and the no-referral rule in writing.
  8. Set up billing and a budget (see LSA billing).
  9. Turn on message leads only if someone will reply within 24 hours. Google recommends that standard. Source: Google Ads Help
  10. Rate every lead each week and report booked appointments, not just lead count.

Frequently asked questions

Can real estate agents use Google Local Services Ads?

Yes. Google lists “Real estate agents and brokers” as a Local Services category, described as agents who help buy, sell or rent property. Availability depends on location, so check eligibility for the client’s area first.

What does Google check for real estate agents on Local Services Ads?

Google’s screening table lists business owner identity verification, license checks for each real estate agent in the firm, professional liability insurance where local law requires it, and business checks for select users.

Do real estate agents need a background check for Local Services Ads?

In states that require a background check to get a real estate license, Google says Local Services doesn’t run a separate one. Google lists 21 such states, including New York, Pennsylvania, Ohio, Michigan and North Carolina.

Can real estate agents target Local Services Ads by ZIP code?

No. Google says real estate agents and brokers in the US and Canada can’t target their ads by postal code. Housing ads in the US and Canada also can’t target by age, gender, parental status or marital status.

Can an agent refer Local Services Ads leads to another agent?

Be careful. Google’s Local Services policy says advertisers can’t sell or pass leads to other businesses, and leads should be fulfilled by the advertiser or its vetted team. Keep LSA leads inside the firm that’s named in the ad.
